1. Abstract

Heat-related illnesses pose increasing health risks, highlighting the need for effective cooling strategies. This project investigates the effects of localised surface cooling on heat transfer and internal temperature reduction within the head and neck. Three complementary methodologies are proposed: Peltier-based cooling experiments, physical thermal phantom testing, and numerical thermal modelling using ANSYS. Preliminary work includes developing a temperature control system, selecting a suitable hydrogel for phantom construction, and exploring steady-state thermal simulations. Subsequent investigations will evaluate the influence of cooling location, intensity, and duration on temperature responses, providing insights to inform the future development of targeted cooling systems.


2. Introduction

The frequency and intensity of extreme heat and heat waves are increasing in the 21st century due to climate change. Between 2000 and 2019, approximately 489,000 heat-related deaths occurred annually, of which 45% were in Asia. [1] A study by Adjunct Assistant Professor Joel Aik from Duke-NUS Medical School reported approximately 40 outdoor workers suffered from heat-related illnesses in Singapore between 2009 and 2023. Of these, approximately 85% experienced severe heat-related illnesses, including heat exhaustion and heat stroke. [2] These figures may be underreported, as workers with milder heat-related illnesses are less likely to seek medical attention.

Heat-related illnesses encompass conditions arising from impaired thermoregulation. Heat stroke is characterised by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C, accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction such as delirium, reduced consciousness, or ataxia. [3] High ambient temperatures, humidity, low wind speeds, and restrictive clothing can impede heat dissipation, increasing the risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke. [1]

The preoptic area of the hypothalamus plays a central role in the body's thermoregulation. It receives temperature information from peripheral and central thermoreceptors and activates thermoregulatory responses to maintain thermal homeostasis, typically within a core body temperature range of 36–38 °C. [4] For heat transfer analysis, the human body can be divided into two thermal regions: the core and the shell. The core comprises deeper body tissues, where most metabolic heat is generated, while the shell consists of the skin and subcutaneous tissues, through which heat is dissipated to the environment. Since heat accumulation can increase core body temperature and contribute to heat-related illnesses, cooling the body's surface is one approach to promoting heat dissipation. In clinical settings, cooling methods include evaporative and conductive cooling, as well as invasive techniques. Common examples include ice-water immersion, ice packs, cooling blankets, and iced lavage. [5]

The risks associated with heat stroke, particularly during strenuous activity in hot and humid environments such as Singapore, motivate the development of preventive cooling systems capable of detecting elevated core body temperatures and delivering targeted cooling. However, understanding how localised surface cooling affects heat transfer and temperature reduction in deeper tissues is essential before such systems can be developed. By characterising these thermal responses, this research aims to provide a foundation for the development of targeted cooling strategies, contributing to future heat-stress mitigation efforts and improved resilience to rising environmental temperatures.


3. Literature Review

There are three main cooling mechanisms: evaporative cooling, contact cooling, and active cooling techniques such as thermoelectric cooling. Evaporative cooling removes heat through the evaporation of a liquid from a surface, while contact cooling involves heat transfer through direct contact with a colder medium. Thermoelectric cooling utilises the Peltier effect, in which an electric current drives heat transfer between the cold and hot sides of a thermoelectric module. This technology is often combined with phase-change materials (PCMs), which absorb or release significant amounts of heat during phase transitions, making them suitable for thermal energy storage and heat management.

3.1. Wearable and Targeted Cooling Technologies

Hamazaki et al. developed AlCool, a wrist-worn device that utilises evaporative cooling to provide a cooling sensation. Alcohol is sprayed onto the wrist, while a fan directed at the sprayed region facilitates evaporation. The device provided a cooling sensation lasting approximately 30 minutes, after which the alcohol had to be reapplied to restore the cooling intensity. [6]

Wang et al. developed a cooling vest using ice as a phase-change material due to its low cost and high latent heat. To address concerns regarding leakage and deformation, they designed a multilayer garment comprising a radiation layer, insulation layer, cooling layer, metal wire fabric, and slow-release heat absorption layer. Testing in an artificial climate chamber demonstrated a reduction of 2.4 °C in average skin temperature at an average cooling rate of 0.04 °C/min, alongside a 0.4 °C reduction in core body temperature. The final core body temperature stabilised at 38 °C, which is 0.5 °C below the threshold for mild heat stroke conditions stated in the study. [7] This is one of the few studies reviewed that reports the effect of surface cooling on core body temperature.

Al-Rjoub et al. developed a head cooling device using ice as a phase-change material for thermal storage, intended for applications such as firefighting, mining, and construction. Water circulates through the system, removing heat from the simulated head and transferring it to an ice-based heat exchanger. The device provided active cooling for 1.5 hours under simulated exercise conditions and 3 hours under resting conditions. [8] Although the study highlights the potential of head cooling to facilitate rapid brain cooling during thermal stress, it does not quantitatively report the temperature reduction achieved.

Localised cooling has also been investigated for temperature management in patients with traumatic brain injury. Lavinio et al. developed an external cervical cooling device for selective brain temperature management. The device consists of a polymeric neck collar containing pouches filled with distilled water, which form the heat exchange interface. The study focused on developing a portable device suitable for out-of-hospital use and evaluating the effects of cervical cooling on venous obstruction and intracranial hypertension. The researchers reported a 29.85% reduction in the brain-core temperature gradient in the cooling group compared with the non-cooling group. [9] This was one of the few studies reviewed that assessed the brain-core temperature differential.

3.2. Localised Cooling using Thermoelectric Technology

Ding et al. developed a flexible phase-change material thermoelectric device (FPCM-TED) in the form of a wristband to provide localised cooling in indoor environments. The PCM combines paraffin and graphene with silicone rubber, allowing a bending radius of 20 mm while maintaining stable performance. The device reduced surface body temperature by 11.21 °C within 30 seconds and maintained the thermoelectric device in a cooled state for at least 300 seconds. [10]

Cao et al. developed a headband for personal thermal regulation using a flexible phase-change material. Copper foam was used as the structural framework, combined with a ternary composite PCM comprising paraffin, styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS), and carbon nanotubes. The flexible PCM achieved a thermal conductivity of 4.92 W/(m·K) and a bending radius of 15 mm. The device was complemented by an application for remote temperature regulation and could maintain the forehead temperature at 32 °C across an ambient temperature range of 5-40 °C. Overall, the device demonstrated a stable temperature reduction of 17 °C for 6 hours. [11]

In addition to PCM-based systems, Mujib et al. developed a wearable head cooling device comprising two Peltier modules, two temperature feedback sensors, two fans, and a heat sink. Powered by a 12 V DC battery and controlled using an Arduino UNO microcontroller, the device provided surface cooling to the forehead region. When the measured temperature exceeded a predefined setpoint, the Peltier modules were activated for 3 minutes before being switched off for 10 minutes. The device was evaluated using usability and stress questionnaires, alongside electroencephalography (EEG) measurements to investigate brain activity and the potential effects of cooling on relaxation and heat-related stress. [12]

Thermoelectric cooling modules have also been integrated with highly thermally conductive thin fins and hydrogel in a cooling helmet design. The fins and hydrogel are arranged radially between the head and the cold surface of the TEC module to facilitate heat transfer across curved and irregular surfaces. The metallic fins provide high thermal conductivity to distribute heat, while the hydrogel's high water content provides high heat capacity and flexibility, reducing thermal resistance caused by air gaps. [13]

3.3. Identified Research Gap

Based on the studies reviewed, evaluations of localised cooling have primarily focused on surface temperature reduction, thermal perception, and physiological responses such as EEG measurements. Although some studies report changes in core temperature or brain-core temperature gradients, systematic investigations of internal heat transfer under varying cooling locations, intensities, and durations remain less extensively investigated.


4. Research Questions, Hypothesis and Scope

This study focuses on the head and neck due to their physiological relevance to thermoregulation and potential for targeted cooling. The hypothalamus, located within the brain, serves as the body's thermoregulatory centre. Additionally, Lavinio et al. [9] investigated cervical cooling, highlighting the proximity of the carotid arteries to the skin and their role in cerebral blood supply. Luo et al. [14] also identified the cheek and posterior neck as regions with high sensitivity to cooling. Although thermal sensitivity does not necessarily indicate greater internal temperature reduction, these findings provide a basis for investigating cooling effectiveness across different head and neck locations.

Based on these findings, the two main research questions for this project are:

  1. How does localised surface cooling influence heat transfer and temperature distribution within the head and neck?
  2. How do cooling location, intensity, and duration influence internal temperature reduction?

The first question investigates heat transfer from deeper tissues towards a cooled surface, while the second examines how cooling location, intensity, and duration affect internal temperature responses. Together, these questions support the following hypothesis:

“Localised surface cooling can reduce internal tissue temperatures, with cooling effectiveness influenced by the location, intensity, and duration of cooling.”

This project aims to characterise internal heat transfer under localised cooling conditions rather than develop a complete wearable cooling device. The focus is on understanding heat transfer between the surface and deeper tissues, rather than developing methods to measure core body temperature. Testing on human subjects under extreme heat stroke conditions will not be conducted due to ethical and safety concerns. Consequently, clinical validation of cooling techniques for heat stroke prevention is beyond the scope of this project.


5. Research Methodology and Preliminary Outcomes

Three complementary approaches have been selected to investigate the research hypothesis. First, active Peltier-based cooling will be used to apply localised surface cooling under controlled conditions. Experiments will compare cooling effectiveness across different locations while evaluating the effects of cooling intensity and duration. Second, numerical thermal modelling will be conducted using ANSYS to simulate these cooling conditions and visualise internal heat transfer. The numerical predictions will subsequently be compared with experimental observations. Finally, since human testing under extreme heat stroke conditions is beyond the project's scope, a physical thermal phantom will be developed to replicate selected thermal properties of human tissues. This will enable controlled investigations of heat transfer mechanisms and internal temperature responses.

5.1. Surface cooling characterization

To evaluate the effects of cooling location, intensity, and duration, an experiment was designed using Peltier modules. These were selected for their ability to actively regulate cooling conditions, making them suitable for controlled and repeatable experiments. Their established use in cooling applications, availability in different sizes, and greater temperature control compared with passive cooling methods such as ice packs further supported their selection.

The ability of a Peltier module to maintain a stable temperature setpoint using closed-loop feedback control was initially evaluated with a proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller. However, the trial-and-error approach used to tune the PID parameters produced noisy and occasionally unstable temperature responses. Consequently, the control system was simplified to on–off control, in which the Peltier module is activated when the measured temperature exceeds the setpoint and deactivated when it falls below it. The experimental setup consisted of a Peltier module with an attached heat sink, an H-bridge driver, an Arduino UNO microcontroller, and a thermocouple with an amplifier unit.

Based on the approximate bilateral symmetry of the human head and neck, five regions were selected for the experiment: the mid-forehead (frontal), right cheek, right temporal region, right lateral neck, and posterior neck (midline). The cheek and posterior neck were selected due to their high sensitivity to cooling, as reported by Luo et al. [14] The lateral neck was included to further investigate the potential influence of cooling near the carotid arteries, as discussed by Lavinio et al. [9] The forehead and temporal regions were selected as additional cooling locations on the head while minimising hair as a potential variable.

During the experiment, the baseline skin surface temperature of each participant will be recorded before cooling is applied. The cooling temperature setpoints will be defined as 5 °C and 10 °C below the baseline temperature to evaluate the effect of cooling intensity. These temperature differences were selected to produce distinguishable cooling responses, as the applied cooling temperature may not directly correspond to the resulting skin temperature. Cooling will be applied for 90 seconds, with surface temperature recorded before, during, and after the cooling period. Subsequent experiments will investigate different cooling durations to evaluate their influence on surface temperature responses and cooling effectiveness. The resulting temperature-time responses and cooling rates will be analysed to investigate the effects of cooling duration. The experimental design was informed by the thermal sensitivity study conducted by Luo et al. [14]

5.2 Physical thermal phantom

Physical phantoms are commonly used to replicate selected properties of human tissues for experimental investigations. Human head phantoms are often designed to simulate electrical properties, particularly for applications involving magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

5.2.1 Existing Tissue-Mimicking Phantoms

Stauffer et al. developed a multilayer tissue phantom for microwave radiometry testing. They analysed a human brain computed tomography (CT) dataset using tissue segmentation software to determine the thicknesses of the scalp, skull, and brain tissues. An artificial human skull with geometry and dielectric properties similar to those of human skull bone was selected and filled with a mixture of ethylene glycol, water, and NaCl to replicate the electrical properties of mixed grey and white matter. A sealed compartment containing temperature-controlled distilled water was also placed against the outer surface to maintain a stable scalp temperature. [15]

Graedel et al. developed an anatomically realistic phantom for radiofrequency heating measurements using geometric data obtained from 1 mm isotropic T1-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) images of a human head to generate a computer-aided design (CAD) model. Highly conductive compartments were filled with tissue-mimicking materials, including agar gel, to minimise convection in heated liquids. Other compartments contained mixtures of agar, salt, sodium azide, polyethylene powder, and deionised water in varying proportions to replicate tissue dielectric properties. [16]

Although these studies provide useful references for the geometric characteristics of head phantoms, their primary focus on dielectric properties makes them less directly applicable to the present investigation. Other phantom models have been developed specifically to replicate aspects of human thermoregulation, although they do not necessarily reproduce the anatomical geometry of the human head.

5.2.2 Thermally Representative Phantoms

Van den Bossche et al. developed an agar-gel block comprising deionised water, glycerol, and agar to replicate the average thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat capacity of human tissue. The thermal properties of fat tissue were represented using an ethyl cellulose-gel block. A water circulation system was embedded within the model to simulate metabolic heat production, alongside a superficial circulation system to facilitate convective heat exchange with the surrounding environment. A sweating system was also incorporated to enhance heat exchange with ambient air, while the gel blocks were wrapped in PVC foil to minimise water evaporation. [17]

The initial objective of this project is to investigate heat transfer primarily through conduction, with convective mechanisms to be incorporated in later stages. Therefore, the complexity of the phantom developed by Van den Bossche et al. makes it less suitable as a starting point. In comparison, the brain phantom developed by Packett et al. for therapeutic cooling devices provides a simpler approach that better aligns with the project's objectives. Their model used a potassium-neutralised superabsorbent copolymer (SAP) of acrylic acid and acrylamide to form a water-based hydrogel. The selection of this material was based on the approximation that the density, thermal conductivity, and specific heat capacity of blood and grey matter are within 20% of those of water at 37 °C, owing to their high-water content. Blood perfusion was simulated using non-directional flow by circulating temperature-controlled water through a container filled with swollen hydrogel crystals. [18]

5.2.3 Proposed Hydrogel-based Thermal Phantom

Since the SAP used by Packett et al. is a proprietary product, poly(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) potassium salt was selected as the hydrogel material for the proposed thermal phantom. This cross-linked superabsorbent copolymer forms discrete swollen crystals when soaked in water. Its selection was guided by its similarity to the material used in [18] and its potential to accommodate simplified blood perfusion in later stages of testing. The experimental setup will consist of a container filled with swollen hydrogel crystals, with thermocouples positioned at 2 cm depth intervals. Surface cooling will be applied to the exposed upper layer, and temperature measurements will be used to characterise heat transfer through spatial temperature gradients. These results will subsequently be compared with numerical predictions from ANSYS.

5.3 Numerical thermal modelling

Numerical thermal modelling using ANSYS aims to visualise and predict heat transfer and internal temperature reduction within the head. For this purpose, a mesh of a human head and neck model was generated in the software. Water at 37 °C was assigned as the model material based on the literature on thermal phantoms. Localised surface cooling will be applied at the previously identified cooling locations. Simulating conditions comparable to those used in the experiments will enable the numerical predictions to be compared with experimental measurements for further analysis.

Initial exploration of ANSYS steady-state thermal modelling demonstrated that applying localised surface cooling to the forehead can produce results such as temperature distributions, total heat flux, directional heat flux along the three coordinate axes, and temperature profiles along predefined paths extending approximately 30 mm from the surface. The temperature profiles are particularly useful for comparison with the spatial temperature gradients obtained from the thermal phantom experiments. Following refinement of the steady-state model and its boundary conditions, transient thermal simulations will be conducted to investigate temperature changes over time under different cooling durations.


6. Future Work and Conclusion

Future work will focus on implementing and refining the three proposed methodologies. Peltier-based cooling experiments will be conducted at the five selected head and neck locations, with cooling intensity and duration varied to evaluate their effects on surface temperature responses. A hydrogel-based thermal phantom testing set-up will be constructed to investigate internal temperature distributions and spatial gradients under controlled cooling conditions. Numerical modelling will involve refining the steady-state model with more realistic boundary conditions, followed by transient simulations to investigate time-dependent heat transfer. Experimental measurements will subsequently be compared with numerical predictions to assess the model's performance.

Overall, this project aims to characterise the effects of localised surface cooling on internal heat transfer through complementary experimental and numerical approaches. The findings are expected to provide a foundation for evaluating cooling effectiveness and informing the future development of targeted cooling systems.
